# Threat model frameworks

> **This report does not select a design.** It is evidence for the modeling
> requirements (#15). It takes no decision and amends no ADR. The decisions it
> informs are already accepted — DEC-001a (ADR-0010), DEC-002 (ADR-0007),
> DEC-003 (ADR-0006) and DEC-005 (ADR-0002) — so where a finding would change
> one of them, it routes to a new ADR that amends it.

## Overview

This report compares threat-modeling frameworks. It is evidence for the modeling
requirements (#15) of tmodel, the project's threat-modeling tool. It
covers ten frameworks: seven methods for analyzing threats to a system
or an organization (§2–§8) and three descriptions of how attackers behave (§9).
§1 compares them in one table, §10 adds shorter entries for related methods and
rating schemes, and §11 sets out what the survey means for tmodel. Each
framework is described on a common set of axes, defined in
[`dimensions.md`](dimensions.md): what it models, its notation, how it
represents attack paths, how far it can be automated, its tooling, its maturity
and adoption, and its strengths and limits. The evidence is the Software
Engineering Institute's 2018 survey of methods, two systematic literature
reviews, the frameworks' primary sources, and the tools' own documentation and
repositories. Searches and sources are logged in [`searches.md`](searches.md)
and [`sources.md`](sources.md); references live in `library/` and are cited by
record id. Where a source was read only in part, or not at all, the text says so.

The main findings, set out in §11, are these. No single method covers the whole
job, so in practice methods are combined: one to find threats, one to show
attack paths, one to score them. An attack path needs three properties: an
order, a shared vocabulary for its steps, and a structure of alternatives (OR)
and requirements (AND) with shared steps. The classic methods each supply part
of this; MITRE Attack Flow combines all three in a published format. Candidate
threats can be generated by rule or by language models, but every method leaves
realism, impact and the choice of mitigation to people. Risk is where the
methods differ most. The field's own gaps concern artificial intelligence (AI)
agents, interchange formats, maintained tools and documentation, and
peer-reviewed evidence. Set against tmodel's accepted core schema, the field
has six things tmodel lacks (G1–G6), among them a system model to analyze, a
link from attack steps to an attack catalog, and a way to import Attack Flow.
Two findings from studies bear on these gaps: manual elicitation misses many
threats, and threat models go stale when systems change.

**Surveys behind this comparison.** SEI's *Threat Modeling: A Summary of Available
Methods* (2018, a technical report) describes twelve methods. A peer-reviewed
systematic literature review (SLR) by Tuma, Calikli and Scandariato (2018)
compares 26 threat-analysis techniques from 38 studies on their applicability,
input, analysis procedure, outcomes and ease of adoption. It finds that
procedures are not precisely defined, outcomes lack quality assurance, and tool
support and validation are limited: 9 of the 26 techniques have no tool at all.
A broader SLR by Xiong and Lagerström (2019, read in abstract only) likewise
observes that "most threat modeling work remains to be done manually". Both
findings feed the gap analysis in §11.

**Sources.** `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`; `tuma-2018-threat-analysis-slr`;
`xiong-2019-threat-modeling-slr` (paywalled: abstract only).

## 2. STRIDE

**What it is.** A threat-discovery framework: a mnemonic for six categories of
threat, each the violation of one security property. It helps find possible
threats against components, data flows, users, services and trust boundaries.
Microsoft's Security Development Lifecycle applies it to each element of a
data-flow diagram ("STRIDE per element", Shostack 2008). Shostack's book *Threat
Modeling: Designing for Security* (2014; not read here) is its standard textbook,
and SEI calls it the most mature threat-modeling method.

The table lists the six categories and the security property each one violates.

| threat | violates |
|---|---|
| **S**poofing | authentication |
| **T**ampering | integrity |
| **R**epudiation | non-repudiation |
| **I**nformation disclosure | confidentiality |
| **D**enial of service | availability |
| **E**levation of privilege | authorization |

**History.** STRIDE was introduced in an internal Microsoft article of April 1999
by Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg (*The threats to our products*), which
recommended it to all Microsoft product teams; Microsoft published the article in
2009. The original article expands the "R" as
"Repudiability" rather than "Repudiation". SEI dates Microsoft's adoption of
STRIDE to 2002. We found no primary source for that year, though Microsoft
published the method in *Writing Secure Code* (Howard & LeBlanc, 2001; second
edition 2002, as listed by Shostack 2008). The first 1999 document is also disputed. Shostack (2008) names it
*Threats to our software*, by Jason Garms, Praerit Garg and Michael Howard,
while the document Microsoft released in 2009 is Kohnfelder and Garg's. Whether
these are one document or two is unresolved.

**Notation.** Not a strict graph language: diagrams plus tables. The system is
drawn as a DFD (external entities, processes, data stores, data flows, trust
boundaries), and threats are recorded in a threat table per element. The Open
Threat Model (OTM) format (`open-threat-model`) carries the same ideas as
assets, trust zones and data flows (RPT-0005).

**How it is applied.** *STRIDE-per-element* checks only the categories that
apply to each element type (e.g. a data store: tampering, information
disclosure, denial of service); *STRIDE-per-interaction* checks each data flow
between elements. The per-element mapping is a rule, which is what lets tools
generate candidate threats from a diagram.

**Attack paths / steps.** Weak to moderate. STRIDE identifies individual threat
types at components and data flows; it has no notion of an ordered sequence of
attacker steps. Chains are added by hand, or by pairing STRIDE with attack trees
(§4), as the hybrid Quantitative Threat Modeling Method (Quantitative TMM, §10)
does.

**Manual vs automatable.** Medium.
- *Automatable:* parsing architecture diagrams; identifying components and data
  flows; applying STRIDE per element; generating candidate threats from
  templates/rules or AI.
- *Manual:* deciding whether a threat is realistic; business impact; choosing
  mitigations; verifying trust boundaries; priority and risk.

**Tooling.** RPT-0003 (#7) compares these tools in detail; the table shows only
each tool's role for STRIDE.

| tool | STRIDE role | license | model format |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/develop/threat-modeling-tool) | generates STRIDE threats from a DFD | free, proprietary | own template/model files |
| [OWASP Threat Dragon](https://github.com/OWASP/threat-dragon) | STRIDE, LINDDUN, CIA (confidentiality, integrity, availability), DIE (distributed, immutable, ephemeral) and PLOT4ai threats on diagrams | Apache-2.0 | JSON |
| [OWASP pytm](https://github.com/OWASP/pytm) | threats generated from a system described in Python code | MIT (its threat catalog reuses material from CAPEC, the Common Attack Pattern Enumeration and Classification, under MITRE's terms) | Python code |
| IriusRisk, ThreatModeler | commercial; STRIDE among supported methods | commercial | see RPT-0003 |

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature and widely taught, documented and tool-supported.
SEI notes that Microsoft no longer maintains STRIDE itself, but it is still used
in the Microsoft Security Development Lifecycle through the Threat Modeling Tool.
High adoption in software security: web and cloud application design,
enterprise architecture reviews, secure software development lifecycle
programs, and developer training.

**Strengths.** Simple, easy-to-teach acronym; a good checklist for beginners;
works well with DFDs; finds common software threats; supported by many tools;
useful early in design; maps directly onto basic security properties.

**Limits.** The number of threats grows quickly as the system gets more
complex, which makes the analysis time-consuming. In a descriptive study with 57
final-year master's students (Scandariato et al. 2015), 19–24% of the threats
produced were incorrect, and 64–69% of the threats in the study's baseline (a
threat-tree catalog) were overlooked: STRIDE misses real threats far more often
than it invents false ones. It does not model attack chains; has no built-in
risk scoring, likelihood or business impact; can produce many generic threats;
depends heavily on diagram quality; can miss domain-specific threats; and is not
suited on its own to privacy (→ LINDDUN, §5), safety, hardware, automotive
(→ TARA, #11) or agentic AI (→ MAESTRO, #12).

**Sources.** `kohnfelder-garg-1999-threats-to-our-products`; `shostack-2008-experiences-threat-modeling-microsoft`; `scandariato-2015-stride-descriptive-study`; `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`; `shostack-threat-modeling-2014` (book, not read: metadata only).

## 3. PASTA

**What it is.** The *Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis*: a
**risk-centric** threat-modeling method created by Tony UcedaVélez (SEI dates it
to 2012), described in full in UcedaVélez and Morana's book (Wiley, 2015). Where
STRIDE asks *what can go wrong*, PASTA asks *which threats matter most to the
business* and works backwards from business objectives to countermeasures. Its
author argues that penetration tests, vulnerability scans and static analysis
each give a partial view, and that a unifying method should connect them to
business impact.

**The seven stages.** The table lists PASTA's stages and the main activities in
each.

| # | stage | main activities |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define objectives | business objectives, security and compliance requirements, business impact analysis |
| 2 | Define technical scope | boundaries of the technical environment; infrastructure, application and software dependencies |
| 3 | Application decomposition | use cases, entry points, actors, assets, trust boundaries, data-flow diagrams |
| 4 | Threat analysis | threat intelligence, security logs and incident data → likely threats |
| 5 | Vulnerability and weakness analysis | existing vulnerability reports; mapping to MITRE CWE and CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures); CVSS or CWSS (Common Weakness Scoring System) scoring |
| 6 | Attack modeling | attack surface; **attack trees**; use and abuse cases; attack → vulnerability → exploit mapping |
| 7 | Risk and impact analysis | business impact, residual risk, countermeasures and mitigation strategy |

**Notation.** Not a notation of its own, but a process that *uses* other
notations inside its stages: data-flow diagrams (stage 3), attack trees and
use/abuse-case diagrams (stage 6), CWE/CVE enumerations and CVSS scores
(stage 5).

**Attack paths / steps.** Strong, through stage 6: attack trees show how attacks
are built, and each attack is linked to the vulnerabilities and exploits it uses.
The author's slides even define an attack tree as the *relationship among
asset, actor, use case, abuse case, vulnerability, exploit and countermeasure* —
close to a graph of tmodel's own object types (ARCH-0001 §3).

**Manual vs automatable.** Low overall. The method is a workshop-heavy process
needing business, architecture, operations and security people (SEI: laborious,
though richly documented). Several *inputs* are automatable — threat-intelligence
feeds, vulnerability scanners, CWE/CVE lookups, CVSS scoring — but tying them to
business impact is human work.

**Risk.** Risk is PASTA's distinguishing feature. The method ends in business
impact and residual risk. Its author argues that risk should include a
**probability** term informed by attack simulation, not only threat ×
vulnerability × impact, though the slides do not define how to compute it.
Relevant to ADR-0006 (DEC-003) and #14.

**Tooling.** No open-source PASTA tool was found (searched 2026-09-29).
VerSprite, the author's firm, offers consulting and tooling, and some commercial
threat-modeling platforms list PASTA among their supported methods (see
RPT-0003).

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature and well documented (a 2015 book); used mainly
by organizations that want threat modeling tied to risk management. SEI notes it
encourages collaboration across stakeholders and has built-in prioritization.

**Strengths.** Ties threats to business impact; covers the whole path from
objectives to countermeasures; built-in prioritization; combines attack trees,
CWE and CVSS rather than replacing them; attacker-centric analysis with
asset-centric output.

**Limits.** Heavy and time-consuming — hard for small teams or fast iterations;
depends on good threat intelligence and on business input; the risk calculation
is not precisely specified in the free sources; its examples are web
applications; the primary sources are written by the method's creator, whose
firm sells PASTA services. **The book itself was not read for this report**
(paywalled); this section rests on the author's 2012 slides and the SEI survey.

**Sources.** `ucedavelez-pasta-owasp-2012`; `pasta-risk-centric-threat-modeling`;
`sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`.

## 4. Attack trees

**What it is.** A method that represents an attacker's **goal** as a tree of the
possible ways to achieve it. If STRIDE is a checklist of what can go wrong,
an attack tree is a map of attacker strategy. Bruce Schneier popularized it in
1999 (*Dr. Dobb's Journal*). SEI calls it one of the oldest and most widely
applied techniques, used on cyber-only, cyber-physical and physical systems.
Originally a method on its own, it is now often combined with STRIDE, CVSS and
PASTA.

**What it models.** Attacker objectives and the alternative ways to reach them:
attack steps, required sub-steps, dependencies between steps, and — when values
are attached to nodes — cost, difficulty, probability or skill. It asks: *what
does the attacker want, how could they get it, what steps are required, and
where can we block them?*

**Notation.** A tree. The root is the attacker's goal (a bad outcome); child
nodes are ways to achieve their parent; leaves are concrete attack steps. Each
goal gets its own tree, so a system analysis produces a *set* of trees; for
complex systems, trees can be built per component. Two node types:

- **OR** — the attacker needs *any one* child;
- **AND** — the attacker needs *all* children.

For example, a tree for the goal *steal user data*:

```
Goal: steal user data                     (OR)
├── Compromise database                   (AND)
│   ├── Gain internal network access
│   ├── Obtain DB credentials
│   └── Query sensitive table
├── Compromise admin account              (OR)
│   ├── Phish admin
│   └── Steal session cookie
└── Access backups                        (AND)
    ├── Find backup location
    └── Obtain cloud key
```

**Attack paths / steps.** Strong — much stronger than STRIDE for attack-chain
thinking, because it shows how steps relate. Two precise points matter for
tmodel:

- **An attack is a set of leaves, not always a single root-to-leaf path.** Under
  an OR node one leaf can suffice (*phish admin*); under an AND node every child
  is needed, so "compromise the database" is the three leaves together.
- **Basic AND nodes are unordered.** A classic attack tree says *which* steps are
  needed, not *in what order*. Ordered steps need an extension such as
  sequential-AND (SAND) attack trees (no library record yet). tmodel's schema
  (ADR-0010) defines an `AttackPath` as an ordered set of `AttackStep`s, so this
  difference has to be handled.

**Manual vs automatable.** Medium; building is manual, analysis is automatable.
- *Manual:* choosing the goal; decomposing it into realistic sub-goals; choosing
  AND/OR; estimating cost, probability, skill, time and impact; choosing
  mitigations; reviewing whether the tree makes sense. SEI notes the method
  assumes high expertise and gives no guidance for assessing sub-goals or risk.
- *Automatable:* candidate trees from known attack catalogs (e.g. MITRE ATT&CK,
  CAPEC); leaves from vulnerability scan results; path cost or risk once values
  are assigned; the cheapest/easiest path; which mitigations block the most paths.

**Tooling.** The table lists the tools considered for attack trees and how far
each supports them.

| tool | kind | notes (checked 2026-09-29) |
|---|---|---|
| [ADTool](https://satoss.uni.lu/members/piotr/adtool/) | academic, free (University of Luxembourg, SnT) | attack–defense trees: adds countermeasure nodes, bottom-up evaluation of values. Described by its authors as free and open source, but the [source repo](https://github.com/tahti/ADTool2) has no license file and was last updated in 2017 — effectively unmaintained |
| [SecurITree](https://www.amenaza.com/securitree-main.php) (Amenaza) | commercial | dedicated attack-tree modeling; generates attack scenarios; ships a library of pre-built trees; also fault-tree analysis |
| [SeaMonster](https://sourceforge.net/projects/seamonster/) | open source, academic (SINTEF) | attack trees and misuse cases; project activity ended in 2016 — unmaintained |
| IriusRisk | commercial threat-modeling tool | no attack-tree editor; its documentation describes reproducing repeatable attack-tree patterns as threat libraries and templates |
| OWASP Threat Dragon | open-source threat-modeling tool | no attack-tree support: data-flow diagrams only; threat trees are an open feature request ([issue #607](https://github.com/OWASP/threat-dragon/issues/607)) |
| draw.io, Mermaid, Graphviz, Visio, Miro | general diagramming | common in practice; no AND/OR semantics or scoring |

Questions asked of each tool: does it support AND/OR nodes, node scoring and
easiest-path calculation; does it export to JSON, XML or YAML; is it open source
and maintained?

**Maturity and adoption.** The concept is mature (over 25 years); tooling
maturity is mixed. Adopted where teams need rigorous reasoning about attacker
behavior — security architecture, high-assurance, embedded, automotive and
hardware security, risk analysis, and academic research. Less used than STRIDE
by everyday web teams, because trees take more effort.

**Strengths.** Excellent for multi-step attacks; shows alternative paths
clearly; AND/OR captures dependencies; supports quantitative scoring; shows
where a mitigation is most useful — e.g. if three paths all need *obtain admin
credentials*, multi-factor authentication blocks all three; can reveal the
easiest attack; fits graph-based visualization; easy for humans to follow.

**Limits.** Trees grow quickly for complex systems; quality depends on expert
knowledge, and paths are missed without domain expertise (SEI: useful only when
the system and its security concerns are well understood); hard to keep current
as the system changes; can over-focus on known goals; scoring is subjective;
needs other models for assets, trust boundaries, vulnerabilities and
mitigations. The **tree shape itself is a limit**. Real systems are graph-like:
one step enables many later ones, so a tree either duplicates shared nodes or
hides shared dependencies. *Attack graphs* (e.g. MulVAL, library record
`mulval`) generalize the idea to graphs. This matters because tmodel is a
knowledge graph that could hold attack trees and richer relationships.

**STRIDE vs attack trees.** STRIDE lists *categories of what can go wrong* and is
better for systematic coverage; attack trees show *how an attacker reaches a bad
goal* and are better for attack paths. They are complementary: Quantitative TMM
builds attack trees for STRIDE categories and scores them with CVSS (§10).

**Sources.** `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`; `schneier-attack-trees-1999`;
`mulval`.

## 5. LINDDUN

**What it is.** A **privacy** threat-modeling framework: the privacy
counterpart to STRIDE. Deng, Wuyts, Scandariato, Preneel and Joosen introduced
it at KU Leuven (*Requirements Engineering*, 2011), and the DistriNet research
unit maintains it at linddun.org. Like STRIDE, its name is a mnemonic for its
threat categories, and it works over a DFD.

**The seven threat types.** The table gives each type's current name and meaning
from linddun.org; the 2011 paper and SEI use older names such as *Linkability*,
*Identifiability* and *Detectability*.

| threat | meaning (linddun.org) |
|---|---|
| **L**inking | associating data items or user actions to learn more about someone |
| **I**dentifying | learning someone's identity through leaks, deduction or inference |
| **N**on-repudiation | being able to attribute a claim to an individual |
| **D**etecting | deducing someone's involvement through observation |
| **D**ata disclosure | excessively collecting, storing, processing or sharing personal data |
| **U**nawareness & unintervenability | not informing, involving or empowering people about their data |
| **N**on-compliance | deviating from best practice, standards or legislation |

Note that **non-repudiation** is a *threat* here but a security *property* in
STRIDE, where *repudiation* is the threat: for privacy, being unable to deny an
action can itself harm the user.

**What it models.** Privacy threats to personal data in a system. The 2011 paper
separates **hard privacy** (data minimization — share as little as possible)
from **soft privacy** (the user must trust the organization holding the data).

**Notation and process.** A DFD of the system; a **mapping table** of which
threat types apply to which DFD element types; a catalog of **privacy threat
tree patterns** that detail how each threat can be realized; threats
documented as misuse cases; finally mapped to **privacy-enhancing technologies**
(PETs) as countermeasures.

**Three flavours today** (linddun.org):
- **LINDDUN GO** — a card deck for a lean team brainstorm from informal sketches;
- **LINDDUN PRO** — systematic analysis of interactions between DFD elements
  with threat trees and mapping tables; described as STRIDE-compatible;
- **LINDDUN MAESTRO** — model-driven, using enriched system descriptions; still
  "more info coming soon". *Not* the Cloud Security Alliance's MAESTRO framework
  for agentic AI, which shares the name.

**Attack paths / steps.** Moderate. Threat trees break a privacy threat into the
conditions that realize it (similar to attack trees), but there are no ordered
steps.

**Manual vs automatable.** Medium, like STRIDE: the threat-to-element mapping is
a rule a tool can apply; judging privacy impact and choosing PETs remain human
work.

**Tooling.** [OWASP Threat Dragon](https://owasp.org/www-project-threat-dragon/)
lists LINDDUN among its threat categories (Apache-2.0); the LINDDUN GO card deck
is available from linddun.org (no license stated). See RPT-0003 for commercial
tools.

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature (2011) and actively maintained, with an
extensive privacy knowledge base (SEI); the standard reference for privacy
threat modeling.

**Strengths.** Brings privacy into the same DFD workflow as STRIDE; reusable
threat-tree knowledge; direct link from threats to privacy-enhancing
technologies; lighter (GO) and heavier (PRO) options.

**Limits.** Same scaling problem as STRIDE — threats multiply with system size;
labor-intensive, and generic threats reduce efficiency (SEI); privacy only, so
used alongside a security method; category names changed since 2011, so sources
disagree on terms; no license stated for its materials.

**Sources.** `deng-linddun-2011`; `linddun-org`; `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`.

## 6. OCTAVE

**What it is.** *Operationally Critical Threat, Asset, and Vulnerability
Evaluation*: a family of **organizational risk assessments** from the CERT
Division of the SEI. It asks: *which information assets matter most to this
organization, what threatens them, and what would it cost us?* It is a
risk-assessment method more than a design-time threat-modeling method.

**History.** From OCTAVE Allegro's own timeline: OCTAVE Framework 1.0 in
**September 1999**, developed with the US Department of Defense (DoD) for
compliance with HIPAA (the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act);
framework 2.0 in 2001; **OCTAVE-S** for small organizations (v0.9 2003, v1.0
2005); **OCTAVE Allegro** in 2007. *The SEI survey says OCTAVE was created in
2003 and refined in 2005; that appears to confuse it with OCTAVE-S (recorded as
a `contradicts` relation in the library).*

**Three variants.** The table shows who each variant is for and how it works.

| variant | for | approach |
|---|---|---|
| OCTAVE method | large organizations | workshops; three phases: asset-based threat profiles → infrastructure vulnerabilities → security strategy and plans |
| OCTAVE-S | small organizations (20–80 people, per SEI) | a small team with good knowledge of the organization; fewer workshops |
| OCTAVE Allegro | information assets | eight streamlined steps; can be done by a small team or one person |

**OCTAVE Allegro's eight steps.** (1) establish risk measurement criteria;
(2) profile the information asset; (3) identify its **containers** — where it
lives: technical (servers, laptops), physical (paper, rooms), people;
(4) identify areas of concern; (5) identify threat scenarios using **threat
trees**; (6) identify risks; (7) analyze risks with a **relative risk score**;
(8) select a mitigation approach.

**Notation.** Worksheets and questionnaires, plus four standard **threat
trees** that classify *where a threat comes from*: human actors using technical
means; human actors using physical access; technical problems (defects,
malware); other problems (natural disasters, power or supplier failure).

**Attack paths / steps.** Weak. Despite the name's similarity to attack trees,
OCTAVE's threat trees classify threat *sources* (actor, access, motive,
outcome); they do not describe multi-step attack paths.

**Manual vs automatable.** Low: judgment-heavy workshops or worksheets; the
impact criteria are defined by the organization itself.

**Risk.** Risk is OCTAVE's strength. Impact criteria are set per organization
(reputation, financial, productivity, safety, legal…), producing a **relative
risk score**; probability is optional because it is hard to quantify. Relevant
to ADR-0006 (DEC-003) and #14.

**Tooling.** Worksheets, questionnaires and guidance in the SEI reports; no
open-source tool found (searched 2026-09-29).

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature and well known in organizational risk
management and compliance; SEI notes it is designed to be scalable.

**Strengths.** Ties security to business impact and the organization's own
risk tolerance; covers people and physical threats as well as technical ones;
the "container" idea captures everywhere an asset lives; Allegro makes it
lighter.

**Limits.** Organizational, not system-design — it does not analyze a
software architecture or data flows; no attack paths; qualitative scoring;
time-consuming; large and vague documentation, per SEI; dated (Allegro is from
2007).

**Sources.** `sei-octave-allegro-2007`; `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`.

## 7. Trike

**What it is.** A formal, automation-oriented threat-modeling method for
security **auditing** from a risk and **defender's** perspective, by Paul
Saitta, Brenda Larcom and Michael Eddington (Trike v1 methodology, 2005, MIT
license). Where STRIDE starts from threat *types*, Trike starts from the
system's **requirements** — what each actor is *supposed* to be able to do — and
treats every deviation from that as a threat.

**How it works (v1).**
1. **Requirements model.** List the actors, the assets, and the intended actions
   on each asset (create, read, update, delete: CRUD), plus any rules. Summarize
   them in an **actor–asset–action matrix**: rows are actors, columns are
   assets, and each cell says, for each CRUD action, allowed, disallowed, or
   allowed with rules.
2. **Implementation model.** Data-flow diagrams and "use flows" showing how the
   intended actions are carried out.
3. **Threat generation — automatic.** Threats follow deterministically from the
   matrix and come in only **two kinds**:
   - **denial of service** — an actor is prevented from an intended action;
   - **elevation of privilege** — an actor does something disallowed, breaks an
     action's rules, or uses the system against another system.

   Trike argues that spoofing, tampering and information disclosure are really
   *attacks* or kinds of elevation of privilege — a direct critique of STRIDE.
4. **Attacks.** Each threat is the root of an **attack tree**; the trees are
   joined into one **attack graph**. Weaknesses, vulnerabilities and mitigations
   are then identified, and reusable **attack libraries** kept.
5. **Risk model.** Asset values, actor risk ratings and action probabilities on
   five-point scales.

**Notation.** The actor–asset–action matrix, data-flow diagrams, and attack
trees/graph.

**Attack paths / steps.** Strong. Trike is the first framework in this report
whose v1 explicitly merges attack trees into an **attack graph**, where one
attack step can serve several threats (the limitation of plain trees noted in
§4). Attack Flow (§9.3) later reached a graph by another route. Trike also draws
a precise line between a **threat** (a business-rule event, never
technology-specific) and an **attack** (a technology-specific step).

**Manual vs automatable.** High by design: once the matrix exists, threat
generation is mechanical. Building the matrix, finding attacks and judging risk
remain human work.

**Tooling.** The Trike tool (v2, Smalltalk, [GitHub](https://github.com/octotrike/trike))
and an older Python port ([GitHub](https://github.com/Dymaxion00/octotrike)), both
MIT-licensed and unchanged since 2019 and 2015 respectively. The project site
(octotrike.org) refuses automated access; the v1 document was read from the
Internet Archive.

**Maturity and adoption.** Niche and dormant. Versions 1.5 (which drops threat
trees) and 2 (with "attack chaining") exist, but v2 was never documented; the
site still says it is under active development, yet its repository last changed
in 2019. SEI calls its documentation vague and insufficient.

**Strengths.** Formal and repeatable; threats come from the system's own access
rules, so coverage is systematic; automatic threat generation; attack graph
rather than isolated trees; clear threat/attack/weakness/vulnerability/mitigation
vocabulary; defender's perspective.

**Limits.** Draft-quality, partly experimental documentation with no worked
example; v2 undocumented and the project dormant; only two threat categories
may feel coarse in practice; SEI finds its five-point risk scales too vague for a
formal method; the actor–asset–action matrix grows quickly for large systems.

**Relevance to tmodel.** Trike v1's model — actor, asset, action, rule, threat,
attack, attack tree/graph, weakness, vulnerability, mitigation, attack library —
is very close to ARCH-0001 §3's object types, and it shows threats generated *by
rule* from a model. Worth reading in full for #15. **No decision is taken here.**

**Sources.** `trike-v1-2005`; `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`.

## 8. VAST

**What it is.** *Visual, Agile, and Simple Threat* modeling: a method created
by Anurag Agarwal (per SEI) and built into **ThreatModeler**, a commercial
threat-modeling platform. Its aim is to make threat modeling scale across a
large organization and fit Agile/DevOps work, rather than be a one-off expert
exercise.

**What it models.** VAST recognizes that development and infrastructure teams
have different concerns, so it uses **two kinds of model** (SEI):
- **application threat models** — the architecture of an application, drawn as
  *process-flow diagrams*;
- **operational threat models** — the infrastructure, drawn as *data-flow
  diagrams* from an attacker's point of view.

The vendor describes it by four characteristics: **scalable**, **automated**,
**integrated** with Agile/DevOps tooling, and **collaborative** across
stakeholders.

**Notation.** Process-flow diagrams and data-flow diagrams, as drawn in the
ThreatModeler tool.

**Attack paths / steps.** Not documented in any public source found. Whatever
the product does is not described as part of the method.

**Manual vs automatable.** High, through the ThreatModeler platform, which the
vendor says automates repetitive tasks. Outside that product there is no public
procedure to automate.

**Tooling.** ThreatModeler only (commercial; compared in RPT-0003, #7). No
open-source implementation found (searched 2026-09-29).

**Maturity and adoption.** A vendor method tied to one product; adoption is
that product's customer base. SEI notes it is explicitly designed to scale but
has **little publicly available documentation**.

**Strengths.** Designed for scale and continuous (Agile/DevOps) use; separates
application and infrastructure views, which match how teams are organized;
built-in automation and prioritization (SEI).

**Limits.** No public specification — steps, inputs, outputs and threat
identification are not documented; vendor-defined and promoted with one
commercial product; the vendor's performance claims come without evidence.
**This section rests on SEI's survey and the vendor's own page only.**

**Sources.** `threatmodeler-vast`; `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`.

## 9. Cyber Kill Chain, MITRE ATT&CK and Attack Flow

Unlike STRIDE and attack trees, which are used to analyze a system being
*designed*, these three describe how *attackers behave*. All came from defenders
studying real intrusions; Attack Flow builds on ATT&CK and can also describe
hypothetical attacks.

### 9.1 Cyber Kill Chain

**What it is.** A model of an intrusion as seven ordered phases, introduced by
Lockheed Martin (Hutchins, Cloppert & Amin, 2011). The paper argues that
conventional defense looks only at vulnerabilities and fails against
persistent, well-resourced adversaries (advanced persistent threats, or APTs);
defenders should study the adversary instead.

The table lists the seven phases and what the attacker does in each.

| # | phase | what the attacker does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reconnaissance | researches and selects the target (e.g. harvests email addresses) |
| 2 | Weaponization | couples an exploit with a backdoor into a deliverable payload |
| 3 | Delivery | transmits it (email attachment, website, USB) |
| 4 | Exploitation | the exploit runs on the victim's system |
| 5 | Installation | installs a backdoor to keep access |
| 6 | Command and Control | the compromised host contacts the attacker |
| 7 | Actions on Objectives | the real goal: steal, destroy, or move further |

**What it models and notation.** The phases of an intrusion, plus a
**courses-of-action matrix**: for each phase, what defenders can do — detect,
deny, disrupt, degrade, deceive, destroy (from US DoD information-operations
doctrine). Indicators seen across intrusions are linked into **campaigns**,
creating an intelligence feedback loop.

**Attack paths / steps.** **Ordered, but coarse.** The adversary must succeed at
every phase, so *one mitigation anywhere breaks the chain*. It is the only
framework so far in this report whose steps have a built-in order, the property
ARCH-0001 §3 asks of an `AttackPath`. But a phase is a stage, not a concrete
action, and real intrusions loop back (e.g. reconnaissance again after gaining
access).

**Manual vs automatable.** Mostly manual analysis of intrusions; the phases are
widely used as labels in threat-intelligence tools.

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature and very widely known; a standard vocabulary
in security operations and threat intelligence.

**Strengths.** Simple; shows defenders they can stop an attack at any phase, not
only at exploitation; ties detection to a defensive action per phase.

**Limits.** Built for malware-delivered network intrusions by external
attackers, so it fits insiders, cloud-account abuse and attacks with no malware
poorly; linear and coarse; a model for analyzing intrusions, not a method for
finding threats in a design.

**Sources.** `lockheed-kill-chain-2011`.

### 9.2 MITRE ATT&CK

**What it is.** A knowledge base of real adversary behavior, maintained by
MITRE since 2013 and built from observed incidents. Its design rationale is
*MITRE ATT&CK: Design and Philosophy* (Strom et al., 2018, revised 2020).

**What it models.**
- **Tactics** — the adversary's *why*: a tactical objective, e.g. Initial
  Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Exfiltration.
- **Techniques** and **sub-techniques** — the *how*, e.g. Phishing (T1566) →
  Spearphishing Attachment (T1566.001).
- **Procedures** — how a specific group actually carried out a technique.
- Linked **groups**, **software**, and **mitigations**; three domains:
  Enterprise, Mobile and ICS (industrial control systems).

**Notation.** A matrix: tactics as columns, techniques in the cells. Also
published as machine-readable STIX 2.1 JSON (`mitre-attack`), which makes it a
ready graph source (RPT-0011).

**How it relates to STRIDE and the Kill Chain.** The source itself answers this
(Design and Philosophy §4.1.3): high-level models such as the Kill Chain and
STRIDE explain adversary goals but not individual actions or how one action
relates to another; exploit and malware databases are too specific. ATT&CK is
the **mid-level** model between them.

**Attack paths / steps.** **Concrete, but unordered.** Techniques are good
candidates for tmodel's `AttackStep`, but tactics are *tags*, not stages — one
technique can serve several tactics, and ATT&CK does not record the order in
which techniques are used. Sequences need something extra: either MITRE's
**Attack Flow** (§9.3), which links techniques by what each one makes possible,
or the Kill Chain's phases, which can supply a coarse order.

**Manual vs automatable.** High for the catalog — it is structured data with
stable IDs. Mapping *your* system's threats to techniques still needs people.

**Tooling.** The table lists the ATT&CK tools and data covered here, with their
licenses.

| tool | what it does | license |
|---|---|---|
| [ATT&CK Navigator](https://github.com/mitre-attack/attack-navigator) | web tool for annotating and coloring the matrix (e.g. coverage, threat profiles) | Apache-2.0 |
| [Attack Flow](https://github.com/center-for-threat-informed-defense/attack-flow) | format and tools for how techniques combine into attacks (§9.3) | Apache-2.0 |
| ATT&CK STIX data | the whole knowledge base as JSON | see `mitre-attack` |

**Maturity and adoption.** Mature and the de-facto industry vocabulary for
adversary behavior; stated uses are adversary emulation, red teaming,
behavioral-analytics development, defensive gap assessment, security
operations center (SOC) maturity assessment, and threat-intelligence enrichment.

**Strengths.** Evidence-based (observed, not hypothesized); stable IDs; very
detailed; linked to mitigations; machine-readable; shared vocabulary across
tools and teams.

**Limits.** Not a threat-modeling method by itself — no process for analyzing a
system under design; covers only behavior someone has already seen and
reported; no ordering of steps; strongest for enterprise IT; changes with each
release.

**Sources.** `mitre-attack-design-philosophy`; `mitre-attack`.

### 9.3 MITRE Attack Flow

**What it is.** A machine-readable language, with open-source tools, for how
adversary actions combine into an attack. It is maintained by MITRE's Center for
Threat-Informed Defense and defined as an extension to STIX 2.1, so flows can
reference other STIX content (threat actors, identities, ATT&CK objects) and be
referenced from it. The extension is labeled 2.0.0 (defined August 2022, released
with the tools' v2.0 in October 2022); the tools reached v4.1 in October 2026.

**What it models.** A flow document holds exactly one `attack-flow` object (name,
description, the starting points, and a `scope`) and four building blocks:
- **actions** (`attack-action`) — one execution of a behavior, usually an ATT&CK
  technique and tactic, with optional start and end times (`execution_start`,
  `execution_end`) and the assets it touches;
- **conditions** (`attack-condition`) — a state or outcome after an action, which
  can branch the flow on success or failure (`on_true_refs`, `on_false_refs`);
- **operators** (`attack-operator`) — `AND` (all incoming paths must succeed) or
  `OR` (any one is enough);
- **assets** (`attack-asset`) — the subject or target of an action, technical or
  not (a machine, data, a person).

`scope` labels what a flow describes: an incident, a campaign, a threat actor,
malware, an emulation plan, an attack tree, or other. The emulation plan and
attack tree values were added to the JSON schema in place (`attack-tree` in 2024,
`emulation-plan` in 2025), while the schema kept its 2.0.0 label; the prose
language page still lists five values. Confidence uses a documented verbal scale
over STIX's 0–100, from "Speculation"
(0, "the author imagines a what-if scenario") to "Certainty" (100).

**Notation.** JSON (STIX 2.1 objects validated by a JSON schema), drawn and edited
as a graph in Attack Flow Builder.

**Attack paths / steps.** **Explicit, and closest to tmodel's model of any method
here.** Actions are concrete steps; operators give AND/OR; conditions give
decision branches; a node can have several incoming arrows, so steps are shared.
An arrow between two actions is a **dependency**: "the second action cannot be
executed until the first action completes successfully" (from the
documentation's introduction). The documentation calls this
"a more powerful concept" than one action merely happening before another: it
records that the first creates the precondition for the second. So an acyclic
flow gives a **partial order**: actions on independent branches stay unordered.
Wall-clock time is optional on each action. Neither the documentation nor the
Builder checks that a flow is acyclic.

Techniques usually come from ATT&CK, but custom collections (e.g. built in
ATT&CK Workbench), other knowledge bases (e.g. VERIS) and ad-hoc actions are
allowed; an action needs only a name.

Attack trees are expressed with these same blocks, not with a separate construct:
the published example with `scope: attack-tree` models goals and sub-goals as
actions without techniques, joined bottom-up through AND/OR operators to one root
goal. There are no cost or probability attributes as in Schneier's trees (§4).

**Manual vs automatable.** The format is fully machine-readable. Writing a flow is
analyst work, usually from an incident report; v4 adds AI-assisted drafting of a
starting flow from a PDF, URL or text report, and technique recommendations. Flows
need not record a real event: emulation plans, attack trees and "what-if"
scenarios are supported, so hypothetical paths — what tmodel produces — fit the
language.

**Tooling.** The table lists the Attack Flow tools and their licenses.

| tool | what it does | license |
|---|---|---|
| [Attack Flow Builder](https://center-for-threat-informed-defense.github.io/attack-flow/ui/) | browser editor (native `.afb` files; publishes STIX JSON; imports STIX since v3.0); views include presentation, treemap, tactic table, timeline, matrix and IOC (indicator-of-compromise) table | Apache-2.0 |
| [Attack Flow repository](https://github.com/center-for-threat-informed-defense/attack-flow) | language schema, Python tooling (the `af` command-line interface, or CLI), corpus of example flows | Apache-2.0 (bundled ATT&CK content under the ATT&CK Terms of Use) |

**Maturity and adoption.** An actively maintained MITRE project with a public
corpus of example flows. ATLAS, D3FEND and Fight Fraud joined ATT&CK in the
Builder in v3.1 (April 2026); v4.0 added AI-assisted drafting, technique
recommendations and tags; v4.1 added D3FEND countermeasures and red-team tests.

**Strengths.** Joins ATT&CK's shared vocabulary with attack-tree structure (AND/OR,
shared steps) and an order derived from dependency, in one machine-readable graph;
supports hypothetical as well as observed attacks; interoperates through STIX 2.1,
which tmodel already plans to import (ADR-0007); open license.

**Limits.**
- Describes attacks, not the system under design: no data-flow model, components or
  trust boundaries, and no risk scoring.
- The 2.0.0 schema changed in place, so a validator holding an older copy rejects
  newer flows; the schema's published URL currently returns 404.
- The Builder stores detections, mitigations, countermeasures and red-team tests as
  custom `x-` objects linked by generic `related-to` relationships, and adds a
  `ttp_frameworks` property. None of these is in the 2.0.0 JSON schema; the
  language page documents `x-detection` and `x-mitigation` only. A STIX importer
  (ADR-0007) must accept custom objects, read `related-to` edges as well as
  `effect_refs`, and cannot learn the ATT&CK version from a flow.
- The Builder's confidence scale (v4) differs from the documented one: it adds "AI
  Generated" at 0 and moves "Speculative" to 10 and "Very Doubtful" to 20, while
  the documented scale has "Speculation" at 0 and "Very Doubtful" at 10. A reader
  using the documented scale misreads Builder values, and nothing in a flow says
  which scale it used. The generation guide also has analysts "validate" AI
  drafts by editing confidence, so confidence carries review state. An importer
  must not derive tmodel's review status (ADR-0010 Review/Assertion) from it.

**Sources.** `mitre-attack-flow`.

### 9.4 Implications for tmodel

Together the three cover what neither the Kill Chain nor ATT&CK has alone: the
Kill Chain gives **order** (coarse), ATT&CK gives **concrete steps** (unordered),
and Attack Flow combines ATT&CK's steps with AND/OR structure and an order
derived from **dependency**. tmodel's accepted core (ADR-0010) defines
`precedes` as an "explicit ordering edge" and puts its AND/OR `gate` on a step's
predecessors. Two readings follow. Read as order, `precedes` says only which
step comes first. Read together with the gate, it can also be taken to mean that
the earlier steps must succeed first, which is Attack Flow's dependency. The
schema does not say which reading holds, and the proposal behind ADR-0010
planned pre- and postconditions on steps that the accepted core left out (gap
G3, §11). Settling it would amend ADR-0010 (DEC-001a). Attack Flow's STIX form
is also a candidate interchange under ADR-0007, with the import caveats above.
**No decision is taken here.**

**Sources.** `lockheed-kill-chain-2011`; `mitre-attack-design-philosophy`;
`mitre-attack`; `mitre-attack-flow`.

## 10. Other methods

The table gives shorter entries of three kinds: methods the SEI survey covers
that are rarely used on their own, combinations of the main methods, and related
frameworks covered elsewhere. Unless noted, the source is
`sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`; the original papers it cites are listed
below for later ingestion.

| method | what it is | attack paths | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Persona non Grata (PnG) | attacker personas — archetypes with skills, motives and goals — that help analysts see the system from the misuse side | none | easy to adopt but rarely used or researched; few false positives but finds only a subset of threat types (SEI) |
| Security Cards | a 42-card brainstorming deck in four dimensions: human impact, adversary motivations, adversary resources, adversary methods | none | good for unusual threats; many false positives; rarely used (SEI) |
| hTMM | SEI's Hybrid Threat Modeling Method (2018): SQUARE (Security Quality Requirements Engineering) + Security Cards + PnG, then a formal risk assessment | none | targets no false positives, no missed threats and consistent results; applied to one cyber-physical scenario (SEI) |
| Quantitative TMM | attack trees built for STRIDE categories per component, then scored with CVSS; "attack ports" pass risk between connected components | yes — component attack trees | Potteiger, Martins & Koutsoukos, 2016; used on a railway network (SEI) |
| CVSS | a severity **score** for vulnerabilities, maintained by FIRST (the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams) — not a threat-modeling method; v3.x has base, temporal and environmental metric groups, v4.0 (2023) base, threat, environmental and supplemental | none | covered by #14 (metrics); library record `first-cvss` (queued, not yet read) |
| DREAD | Microsoft's risk-rating mnemonic (Damage potential, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users, Discoverability); per SEI, the first four are rated 0, 5 or 10 and Discoverability 0, 5, 9 or 10, then averaged | none | a rating scheme, not a method; SEI cites a Microsoft engineer's critique of it (LeBlanc, *DREADful*, 2007) — see #14 |
| ISO/SAE 21434 TARA | the automotive threat analysis and risk assessment method (asset → threat scenario → attack path → attack feasibility → risk) | yes — attack paths with feasibility ratings | covered by #11; library record `iso-sae-21434-2021` |
| MAESTRO (Cloud Security Alliance, 2025) | threat modeling for **agentic AI**: a seven-layer reference architecture (foundation models → agent ecosystem) with layer-specific and **cross-layer** threats | cross-layer threats, no path notation | extends rather than replaces frameworks like STRIDE, for AI agents; central to #12; library record `csa-maestro-2025`. Not the same as LINDDUN MAESTRO (§5) |

**Observations.**
- **PnG and Security Cards** are brainstorming aids about *who* attacks and
  *why*; they add the attacker's motives and resources, which none of the main
  methods model explicitly.
- **hTMM and Quantitative TMM** confirm the pattern seen throughout this report:
  in practice the methods are **combined** — one to find threats, one to show
  attack paths, one to score them.
- **MAESTRO** is the only method here designed for AI agents; the others predate
  them (SEI's survey is from 2018).

**Original sources to ingest later** (cited by SEI): Cleland-Huang, *How Well Do
You Know Your Personae Non Gratae?*, IEEE Software 31(4), 2014 (PnG); Mead &
Shull, *The Hybrid Threat Modeling Method*, SEI Blog, 2018 (hTMM); Potteiger,
Martins & Koutsoukos, *Software and attack centric integrated threat modeling
for quantitative risk assessment*, HotSoS 2016, DOI 10.1145/2898375.2898390
(Quantitative TMM).

**Sources.** `sei-threat-modeling-methods-2018`; `csa-maestro-2025`;
`first-cvss`; `iso-sae-21434-2021`.

## 11. Implications for tmodel

Evidence for #15 (modeling requirements), #14 (metrics) and DEC-001. **No
decision is taken here** — this section only states what the survey shows.

**1. No single method covers everything; in practice they are combined.** Each
method answers a different part of the job; the table shows which methods do
each part well.

| job | methods that do it well |
|---|---|
| find threats systematically | STRIDE (security), LINDDUN (privacy), Trike (from access rules) |
| show how an attack unfolds | attack trees, Trike's attack graph, Kill Chain, Attack Flow, TARA's attack paths (ATT&CK supplies the steps, unordered) |
| judge how bad it is | PASTA, OCTAVE, Trike's risk model, ISO/SAE 21434 TARA; CVSS as a score (#14) |
| widen the attacker view | Persona non Grata, Security Cards |

The hybrid methods make this explicit: Quantitative TMM = attack trees + STRIDE
+ CVSS; hTMM = SQUARE + Security Cards + PnG; PASTA uses DFDs, CWE, CVSS and
attack trees inside its stages. A tool that supports only one method would
cover only part of the job.

**2. Attack paths need three properties; the classic methods each cover part, and Attack Flow combines all three in a published format.**
tmodel's accepted schema (ADR-0010) defines an `AttackPath` as "an ordered set of
AttackSteps". The table shows where each of the three properties comes from and
what each source lacks.

| property | where it comes from | gap |
|---|---|---|
| **order** | Cyber Kill Chain (seven ordered phases); Attack Flow (dependency orders actions) | Kill Chain phases are coarse, not concrete steps |
| **concrete, shared vocabulary for steps** | MITRE ATT&CK techniques (stable IDs, machine-readable), also used by Attack Flow; CAPEC attack patterns, which tmodel's own example models use (all 7 `AttackPattern`s in `spec/vectors/` are CAPEC ids) | ATT&CK tactics are tags; no order. CAPEC's execution flow and its `CanPrecede`/`CanFollow` relations are the nearest released analogue of ordered steps (RPT-0005), but CAPEC is not described in this report (`capec`) |
| **structure — alternatives (OR) and requirements (AND), shared steps** | attack trees; Trike's attack graph; Attack Flow (AND/OR operators, shared nodes) | plain trees have no order and duplicate shared steps |

Existing work already combines them: MITRE's **Attack Flow** has all three in one
STIX graph (§9.3); sequential-AND attack trees add order to trees (no source is
recorded for them yet); Trike merges trees into a graph; logical attack graphs
such as MulVAL (`mulval`, §4) combine order and AND/OR structure over
vulnerability-level steps. A graph model, which tmodel is, can hold all three.
This qualifies RPT-0005's G2 ("no released format has an ordered attack path"),
which did not consider Attack Flow.

**3. Existing object models overlap tmodel's accepted core (ADR-0010), with gaps.**
The working labels of ARCH-0001 §3 are superseded by ADR-0010's class names, and
the accepted core has no asset class, no actor–action pair and no use or abuse
case (gaps G1 and G5, item 7). Trike v1 defines actor, asset, action, rule,
threat, attack, attack tree/graph, weakness, vulnerability, mitigation and
attack library; PASTA's author defines an attack tree as the relationship among
asset, actor, use case, abuse case, vulnerability, exploit and countermeasure.
Both are worth reading in full for #15. Trike also draws a useful line: a
**threat** is a business-level event, an **attack** a technology-specific step.

**4. Threats can be generated by rule; judgment stays human.** STRIDE-per-element,
LINDDUN's mapping table and Trike's actor–asset–action matrix all generate
candidate threats mechanically from a model. Language models are a second route:
STRIDE GPT (`stride-gpt`, MIT license) generates STRIDE threats and attack trees,
and Attack Flow v4 drafts flows from incident reports (§9.3); rules and models
can also be combined. Every method still leaves realism, impact and the choice
of mitigation to people. This is consistent with tmodel's position that
machine-proposed threats are hypotheses until a human reviews them (ARCH-0001
§2, §7).

**5. Risk is where the methods differ most.** PASTA and OCTAVE tie risk to
business impact set by the organization; Trike uses five-point scales; CVSS
scores vulnerabilities, not threats; ISO/SAE 21434 rates attack feasibility
(#11). Input to #14 and to ADR-0006 (DEC-003, accepted).

**6. Gaps.**
- **AI agents:** of the methods surveyed here, only MAESTRO (2025) targets them.
  RPT-0014 covers the wider field (OWASP's agentic threats and mitigations guide,
  MITRE ATLAS, NIST AI 100-2, CoSAI's risk map) → #71.
- **Interchange:** the methods themselves define no file format; their tools use
  their own. Openly published formats sit beside them: Attack Flow's STIX 2.1
  extension (§9.3); OTM (`open-threat-model`), which ADR-0007 commits tmodel to
  import; and CycloneDX 2.0's threat-model modules (TM-BOM: threats, attack trees,
  ordered attack paths), merged but unreleased. RPT-0005 surveys these formats
  (its G2, G12–G14) and finds no released format with an ordered attack path. It
  did not consider Attack Flow, whose arrows do order actions (§9.3), so its G2
  should be read together with this report.
- **Documentation and tools:** Trike is dormant, VAST is vendor-only, and several
  attack-tree tools are unmaintained (SeaMonster since 2016, §4), so several methods
  cannot be reused directly. RPT-0003 finds the closest maintained tools to
  reviewable attack paths in ThreatTree and STRIDE GPT's generated attack trees.
- **Evidence:** SEI's comparison (2018) is qualitative and contains factual
  errors (OCTAVE's date, §6; the unsupported "adopted in 2002" for STRIDE, §2).
  Peer-reviewed evidence is thin but exists: a systematic literature review
  (Tuma et al. 2018), a descriptive study of STRIDE (Scandariato et al. 2015), a
  replicated controlled experiment on STRIDE's variants (Mbaka & Tuma 2024), and
  controlled comparisons of attack trees with misuse cases (Karpati et al. 2014)
  and of attack graphs with fault trees (Lallie et al. 2018). Lallie et al. (read
  in abstract only; 63 participants) found that an adapted attack graph aided the
  perception of an attack better than a fault tree. Karpati et al. is recorded but
  not read (no abstract available). In practice, interviews in seven large Dutch
  organizations (Verreydt et al. 2024) found that none reassess threat models when
  the system changes, "mostly due to the lack of tool support". Item 7 draws on
  Scandariato et al., Mbaka and Tuma, and Verreydt et al.

**Evidence bearing on these gaps.** Two findings are evidence rather than
capabilities, so they sit outside the table.
- **Manual elicitation misses much.** With STRIDE, 64–69% of the threats in a
  threat-tree baseline were overlooked (Scandariato et al. 2015), while 19–24% of
  the threats produced were incorrect; in the STRIDE replication, precision was
  only 0.40–0.43 (Mbaka & Tuma 2024). The two studies measure precision
  differently, and both used students. Scandariato's baseline is itself a
  catalog, roughly what a rule engine would enumerate, so "generate
  candidates by rule" is not proven by them. Generation also
  multiplies candidates, and R-018 requires a human to review each one.
  Options: rule-based generation over G1's model; language-model generation
  (item 4); or both, with every candidate kept `proposed` until reviewed.
  Routes to #15, R-018, #63.
- **Models go stale.** None of seven large Dutch organizations reassesses threat
  models when the system changes, "mostly due to the lack of tool support"
  (Verreydt et al. 2024). tmodel keeps files as the source of truth and
  recomputes on request (ADR-0004, #63), but nothing triggers a recomputation
  when the model or product changes. Option: a CLI check that recomputes and
  reports changed paths, usable in continuous integration. Routes to #47,
  PLAN-0002.

**What the accepted core already holds.** It holds order, AND/OR structure and
shared steps in one graph (G2 still needs the catalog link that Attack Flow
has). It keeps machine proposals apart from human review through `Assertion`
and `Review` (ADR-0010), whereas Attack Flow v4 marks AI-drafted content only
through its confidence value. And it has a risk vector, which follows ISO/SAE
21434's method (ADR-0006).

## Conclusion

No threat-modeling method does the whole job. STRIDE, LINDDUN and Trike find
threats systematically; attack trees, Trike's attack graph, the Cyber Kill
Chain, Attack Flow and TARA's attack paths show how an attack unfolds; PASTA,
OCTAVE, Trike's risk model and TARA judge how bad it is; and Persona non Grata
and Security Cards widen the view of the attacker. In practice the methods are
combined, so a tool that supports only one would cover only part of the job.

For attack paths, three properties matter: order, a concrete and shared
vocabulary for steps, and AND/OR structure with shared steps. The Kill Chain
gives a coarse order, ATT&CK gives concrete but unordered steps, and plain
attack trees give AND/OR structure without order. MITRE Attack Flow combines all
three in one published, machine-readable graph, which qualifies RPT-0005's G2.
The object models of Trike v1 and PASTA overlap tmodel's accepted core, which
has no asset class, actor–action pair or use or abuse case. Candidate threats
can be generated by rule or by language models, but realism, impact and the
choice of mitigation stay with people. Risk is where the methods differ most.

The field has gaps of its own: of the methods surveyed here, only MAESTRO
targets AI agents; the methods define no file formats of their own; several
methods and tools are dormant, vendor-only or unmaintained; and peer-reviewed
evidence is thin, though it exists.

Measured against tmodel's accepted core, the field has six things tmodel lacks:
a system model of DFD elements and trust boundaries (G1); attack steps linked to
a catalog such as CAPEC or ATT&CK (G2); a stated meaning for `precedes`, and
branching on success or failure (G3); separate ratings for a path's
alternatives (G4); an asset class and business impact (G5); and a way to import
Attack Flow (G6). Two findings from studies bear on these gaps: manual
elicitation misses many threats, and threat models go stale when systems
change. The accepted core already holds order, AND/OR structure and shared
steps in one graph, keeps machine proposals apart from human review, and has a
risk vector that follows ISO/SAE 21434.

The report takes no decision. Each gap's options route to the owner named in
§11, and a change to an accepted decision routes to a new ADR.
