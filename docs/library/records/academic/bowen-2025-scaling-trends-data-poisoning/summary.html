Overview

The paper asks whether data poisoning gets worse as language models grow, and finds that it does for most model families. It mixes a small share (0.5% to 2%) of harmful examples into 5,000-example fine-tuning sets for three threat models: malicious fine-tuning that strips refusals (Harmful QA); imperfect curation that skews political sentiment with no attacker involved (Sentiment Steering); and deliberate contamination that plants a sleeper-agent backdoor, code with a cross-site scripting flaw whenever the prompt says the year is 2025 (Code Backdoor) (§3, §4). Across 24 open-weight models from 8 families, 1.5 to 72 billion parameters, fine-tuned with 4-bit QLoRA, larger models learn the poisoned behaviour faster. The trend is statistically significant for Harmful QA, and for Sentiment Steering after four or five epochs; it points the same way for Code Backdoor, while Gemma-2 likely scales the other way (§7). On OpenAI's fine-tuning API, every GPT model tested learned harmful behaviour from Harmful QA, though moderation then disabled the GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini versions. A 2% share of harmful examples rewritten with the Skeleton Key jailbreak passed moderation and produced a GPT-4o that refused 4% of StrongREJECT prompts, against 94% before (§6, §6.1).

Limits

  • The poisoning rates are high for the web threat. The lowest rate tested is 0.5% of a 5,000-example set. Poison planted on the web would be orders of magnitude rarer, and the check that the trend holds at lower rates is exploratory (§8, Appendix F).
  • LoRA, not full fine-tuning. Every open-weight run uses 4-bit QLoRA at rank 16. The authors expect full fine-tuning to agree but did not test it (Appendix G).
  • Model judges and string matching do the scoring. GPT-4o mini scores harmfulness and bias through StrongREJECT-style rubrics, and a code answer counts as vulnerable if it contains autoescape=False (Appendix B).
  • The bias experiment is simulated. Its news articles are Claude 3 imitating a commentator's answers to 150 questions about one politician (§4.2).
  • The moderation results are a 2024 snapshot. OpenAI's moderation blocked inconsistently, including runs with no poison at all, and its internals are not public (Appendix C, Table 5).
  • No pretraining poisoning, and no safety training afterwards. The contamination threat model is motivated by scraped web content and by sleeper agents, but the experiments only fine-tune, and persistence through later safety training is cited, not tested (§4, §8).
  • Narrower than earlier versions. v6 drops the jailbreak-tuning study that v4 and v5 carried (now arXiv:2507.11630). NIST cites the paper under its v1 to v3 title, for a point v6 still makes.
