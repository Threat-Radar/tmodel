Overview

Multimodal contrastive models such as CLIP learn from image-caption pairs scraped from the Internet with no human review, and the paper argues that this makes poisoning a practical threat: anyone who can post images online can place a few pairs in the training set (§1, ethics statement). It adapts standard targeted poisoning and patch-trigger backdoors to contrastive training. A target image is paired with many captions for the desired label, or many trigger-patched images with such captions, and the caption set is kept diverse so that the image encoder, not the text encoder, learns the poison (§3). With open-source CLIP code on Conceptual Captions (3 million pairs) and a 15-million-pair YFCC subset, three poisoned pairs make a chosen image classify as a chosen ImageNet class 40% of the time in zero-shot use, and poisoning 0.01% of the data gives a 50% backdoor success rate, with no significant drop in clean accuracy (§4). Ablations with a lower-variance metric, the backdoor z-score, find that a fixed 300 poisons keep working as the dataset grows from 50,000 to 3 million pairs, and that larger models are easier to backdoor (§5).

Limits

  • Getting the poison into a crawl is argued, not shown. The experiments add the poisoned pairs to the dataset directly (§4.1). How an attacker lands them in a scraped dataset is left to the claim that the data is uncurated (§1); S-0231, AIT-013's other source, is where routes such as expired domains are demonstrated.
  • Small scale by today's standards. A ResNet-50 CLIP on 3 million and 15 million pairs, with ablations on shrunken models and datasets because larger runs were too expensive (§5.1.2). Behaviour at LAION scale or with much larger models is extrapolation.
  • Image encoder only. The authors say the text encoder can also be attacked but report no results for it (§2.3).
  • No defense is evaluated. Duplicate removal is dismissed in one paragraph, because added noise or word substitutions evade it (§3.1). Filtering defenses are left to future work (§6).
  • One effect is unexplained. For backdoors, the zero-shot model can be vulnerable while a linear probe trained on its features is not (§4.3).
  • No deployed system was attacked. The ethics statement says such classifiers were not yet in security-critical use, so the results are laboratory results.
