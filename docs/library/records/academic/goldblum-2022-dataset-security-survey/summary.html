Overview

The survey starts from the point that modern datasets are harvested from unverified sources, so training data is an open door: attackers can leave poisoned content on the web for scrapers, submit it to systems that learn from their users, or control their own devices in federated learning (§1). It sorts the attacks into training-only poisoning, which needs no access at test time and is grouped by method (feature collision, bilevel optimization, influence functions, label flipping, online and federated poisoning), and backdoor attacks, which plant a trigger at training time and use it at test time (§2, §3, Figures 1 and 2). It classifies defenses by stage: detecting poisoned data or models, repairing models after training, and preventing poisoning during training, with separate defenses for federated learning (§4, Figure 4). Each part ends with open problems, among them backdoors that survive full fine-tuning, attacks with limited knowledge of the dataset, defenses beyond image classification and certified defenses at realistic scale (§2.8, §3.4, §4.6).

Limits

  • Coverage ends in early 2021. It predates instruction-tuning, preference-data and web-scale language-model poisoning, which RPT-0014 covers from other sources (AIT-017, AIT-018, AIT-014).
  • Image classification dominates, above all for defenses, as the authors themselves note (§4.6).
  • No new experiments. Effectiveness claims are the cited papers' own. The survey itself notes that a benchmark found many poisoning attacks weaker than advertised once the victim's training settings differ (§2.8).
  • The authors' own work is prominent. Several reviewed methods come from their groups, among them MetaPoison, Witches' Brew, the poisoning benchmark and the data-augmentation defense, and the conclusion says the open problems reflect the authors' interests (§5).
  • Preprint, not the version of record. arXiv v4 was read; the TPAMI version was not compared.
